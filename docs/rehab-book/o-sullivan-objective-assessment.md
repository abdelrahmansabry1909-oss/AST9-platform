# The Objective Assessment Framework for Movement Strategy Analysis

The Objective Assessment Framework focuses on moving beyond a simple diagnosis to identify the "why" behind a patient's movement. By matching objective findings with the patient's subjective story, therapists can identify motor adaptations and design targeted treatment plans.

## 3.1 The Three Critical Questions

During the assessment, therapists should ask themselves three questions to help define the root problem rather than just labeling symptoms.

### Q1: "What are the possible reasons I am noticing the patient moving this way to achieve that task?"

- **Reasoning:** This connects the movement intent with previous experiences or stressors.
- **Example:** A patient failing a toe-touch might not depress their ribcage because a history of prolonged stress has sensitized their respiratory system, shortening the diaphragm and preventing the low back muscles from relaxing.

### Q2: "What isn't happening that, if it did, would cause the symptoms to either reduce or disappear altogether?"

- **Reasoning:** This identifies missing components or inactive tissues that should be sharing the load.
- **Example:** If the patient's ribcage depressed more or their diaphragm lengthened, it would facilitate low back relaxation and potentially decrease the pain experience.

### Q3: "What is happening that, if it stopped happening, would cause the symptoms to reduce or disappear altogether?"

- **Reasoning:** This focuses on compensatory strategies or tissues doing too much work.
- **Example:** If the low back muscles stopped doing "excessive work" to stabilize the trunk during the toe-touch, the pain would likely reduce.

## 3.2 The Three-Layer Objective Assessment

This structured approach builds a progressively stronger hypothesis by "zooming in" on movement strategies.

### Layer 1: Generic Objective Assessment

A 30,000-foot overview of big movements (e.g., toe-touch, side flexion) performed without active cueing. The goal is to see if the patient moves with "thoughtless, fearless movement".

**Challenging the Base of Support:** This principle involves observing how the nervous system reacts to keep the center of gravity balanced. For instance, in right side flexion, the therapist looks to see if the left hip travels outside the foot to challenge the base of support; if it doesn't, it suggests a lack of load tolerance on that side.

### Layer 2: Passive Assessment

The therapist zooms in on specific motor adaptations and joint ranges. Following second-order thinking, therapists avoid treating the first adaptation found (like a tight hip) unless it is cross-linked to the subjective story, as it may just be a reaction to a deeper stressor.

### Layer 3: Coordinative Testing

This layer stresses the nervous system directly using submaximal loads (40–50% effort) to assess force steadiness. It identifies "perceived threats" by seeing how the body self-organizes to distribute force.

### Cross-linking Principle

Findings from all three layers must match the subjective story (previous injuries / stressors); if they don't, the therapist may be on a "wild goose chase".

## 3.3 Movement Strategy Analysis

This framework analyzes how the nervous system protects the body through habituated patterns.

### Motor Adaptations

These are protective strategies the nervous system implements when a noxious stimulus or pain is present. Key findings show that once a stimulus is removed, these protective behaviors often remain, requiring active "undoing" through rehab.

### Co-contractions

- **Peripheral:** Necessary for stability (e.g., soleus and hamstrings co-contracting at the knee) to allow forces to transfer to the hip.
- **Central:** Perceived threats (like an old ankle injury) can cause the nervous system to "stiffen" the ribcage and low back as a protective central co-contraction.

### Identifying Work

Tissues in the painful area are usually doing too much work, while the true root cause is often found in tissues that are not doing enough.

### Load Tolerance vs. Tension

- **Load tolerance** is the physical and mental capacity to handle stress.
- The ability to tolerate **tension** is the specific starting point for graded exposure; by helping tissues tolerate lengthening, they provide essential sensory information to the spinal cord to achieve "thoughtless, fearless movement".

### Where to Start

Treatment starts by using KPIs (Key Performance Indicators) to identify which tissues, when desensitized or loaded, immediately improve movement quality. Following the 80/20 rule, therapists spend 80% of their time on the true driver (identified via the subjective story) and 20% on the symptoms.
